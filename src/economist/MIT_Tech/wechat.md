# Hinton Warns AI Will Replace Human Jobs, LeCun Pushes Back: First Listen to These 5 People

Over the past six months, the debate over whether AI will take our jobs has intensified. Almost every few weeks, a new research report, industry forecast, or anxious story has been pushed into the spotlight. Tech companies talk about "AI empowering everyone" on one hand, while quietly freezing hiring or carrying out mass layoffs on the other. Among white-collar workers, the worry that "my job may only have a few years left" has been spreading.

Recently, the "godfather of AI," Geoffrey Hinton, also weighed in on this issue in an interview. As one of the founders of deep learning and the 2024 Nobel Prize winner in Physics, Hinton has continued to warn about the potential risks of AI since leaving Google.

## Hinton's View

If we look back at human history, every major technological revolution has followed a similar rhythm: one type of labor is eliminated while new employment space is opened up. Agricultural mechanization freed workers from the land and sent them into factories. After factory automation, people moved into office and knowledge work. Economies have always been able to generate new layers of demand to absorb labor displaced by older technologies. But AI is different. It is not a machine sitting behind one door. It appears behind every door.

After a customer-service worker loses their job and retrains as a programmer, AI can also write code. If they then move into legal document work, AI can do that too. If they try content creation, AI is there as well. Each transition may be caught up before it has time to stabilize. Hinton has taken this judgment to a civilizational level: throughout history, humanity has repeatedly broken through constraints. Food scarcity was solved by agriculture, distance by transportation, and physical limits by machines. Each breakthrough created a new space for human activity. AI is now breaking through human intelligence itself. If intelligence is no longer the bottleneck, where is the new space?

Hinton admits that he does not currently have an answer, and he has not seen anyone provide a convincing one. He also offered a concrete timeline: by 2026, AI will largely replace human customer-service workers in call centers; within a few years, it may be capable of independently running software engineering projects for months at a time. These remarks quickly triggered discussion.

## LeCun's Response

Not everyone agrees with Hinton. Not long after, another Turing Award winner, Yann LeCun, responded publicly. He was blunt: "I respect Geoff [Hinton], but on the question of how technological revolutions affect labor markets, he knows less than Dario Amodei [CEO of Anthropic]."

He then made a more provocative point: do not listen to AI scientists on this issue, no matter how brilliant they are; even less should we listen to AI company CEOs, no matter how successful they may be. Questions about labor economics should be answered by scholars who study labor economics.

He then tagged five economists:

- Philippe Aghion
- Daron Acemoglu
- Erik Brynjolfsson
- Andrew McAfee
- David Autor

## Why These Five?

The value of LeCun's post is not just that it takes a position. It also reveals a clear imbalance in public discourse. On the question of whether AI will replace human work, the loudest voices are still usually two groups: AI technologists and tech executives. The former understand model capabilities deeply, but may not have the professional tools to judge how an economic system absorbs technological shock. The latter have both a business incentive to emphasize AI's transformative power and a motive to downplay its social costs.

Meanwhile, economists who have spent decades studying labor markets, building methods, and collecting empirical data have a much weaker public presence. So what do the five scholars LeCun named actually think about human coexistence with AI? Let's take them one by one.

## Five Economists at a Glance

| Scholar | Affiliation | Core View | Keywords |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daron Acemoglu | MIT Economics | Cautious on AI's economic impact, which he thinks is overstated | `so-so technology`, low productivity gains |
| David Autor | MIT | More constructive; emphasizes task reallocation and augmentation | task-based analysis, democratization of expertise |
| Erik Brynjolfsson | Stanford HAI / Digital Economy Lab | Opposes a substitution-first design mindset; argues for augmenting humans | `Turing trap`, augmentation |
| Andrew McAfee | MIT Sloan / IDE | Technologically optimistic, but anxious about distribution | winner-take-all, institutional anxiety |
| Philippe Aghion | College de France / LSE | Believes in creative destruction, but only if institutions can absorb it | creative destruction, Schumpeterian growth |

## 1. Daron Acemoglu: The Most Cautious Skeptic

Daron Acemoglu may be the most cautious of the five when it comes to AI's economic effects. He teaches in the economics department at MIT and won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics for his pioneering contributions to institutional economics. In recent years, he has shifted much of his research toward AI. In 2024, he published "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI," whose conclusion was surprising: according to his model, AI will raise U.S. total factor productivity, a core measure of an economy's overall efficiency, by only about 0.5% to 0.9% over the next decade. That is almost an order of magnitude below Silicon Valley's popular story that AI will reshape the global economy.

But Acemoglu does not think AI is worthless. He argues that at present, AI can automate only a small share of all tasks in firms, and many automated steps are not being replaced by new, more productive tasks. He describes this kind of technology as "so-so technology": good enough to replace workers, but not good enough to deliver commensurate productivity gains. Workers are pushed out, while the economy as a whole does not benefit much.

At the same time, he is not worried that AI will become so powerful that humans will have nothing left to do. Quite the opposite: he thinks the problem may be that AI is not powerful enough. Firms have an incentive to replace labor with it precisely because it is cheap, even if the productivity gains are tiny.

In April 2026, he released a new NBER study on what happens when AI-generated content becomes training data for future AI models. The concern is not only that the model may deteriorate, but also that human knowledge itself may degrade systematically. Because AI tends to produce the most probable, middling answer, it can dilute extreme cases and original thinking in human civilization, causing us to lose the ability to handle complex or rare problems as we become more dependent on AI.

## 2. David Autor: An Optimist Through the Lens of Tasks

David Autor, also a professor at MIT, is one of the most-cited labor economists in the world. Compared with Acemoglu, Autor's perspective leaves more room for constructive possibility. His most influential analytical framework is known as task-based analysis: instead of treating a job as a single unit and deciding whether it survives or disappears, one breaks it down into individual tasks and examines which will be automated and which will actually be enhanced. In this framework, most jobs do not simply vanish or survive. They undergo structural drift: some tasks are handed over to machines, while others become more important because of machine support.

In 2024, Autor made a counterintuitive argument: AI may be beneficial for the middle class. His reasoning goes like this. Over the past few decades, the threshold for professional skill has kept rising. Doctors, lawyers, and advanced engineers have built walls that less skilled workers cannot easily cross, thanks to years of training and judgment. But if AI is designed as an assistive tool rather than a replacement, it may allow some "expert-level judgment" to become accessible to a broader population. A community nurse using an AI diagnostic system can handle preliminary screening that used to be done only by specialists. A small business owner using AI legal tools can review contracts that once required a lawyer.

Autor summarizes this as the democratization of expertise. The point is not to eliminate experts, but to lower the barrier to becoming a "quasi-expert." Of course, he also repeatedly stresses that this is not an automatic result of technological progress. It depends on how firms deploy the technology and how policy steers it. In February 2026, he and Acemoglu co-authored "Building Pro-Worker AI," which systematically defines what counts as worker-supportive AI and tries to give technology development a clearer value orientation.

These technologies include:

- "New task creation" technologies that open up entirely new forms of work
  - For example, turning farmers into "precision agriculture coordinators" who manage multidimensional data
- "Decision support" technologies designed to lower barriers to entry
  - For example, AI tools that help nurses carry out complex clinical diagnoses and spread medical expertise more widely
- "Collaborative human-in-the-loop" systems that keep humans at the center of the loop
  - For example, precision manufacturing setups where AI handles error correction while workers make the core process decisions

## 3. Erik Brynjolfsson: Against the Turing Trap

The third scholar, Erik Brynjolfsson, directs Stanford's Digital Economy Lab and is a senior fellow at Stanford HAI. As early as 2014, he co-authored *The Second Machine Age* with Andrew McAfee, anticipating the profound impact of digital technology on the labor market. The book remains an important reference in this field.

But his most distinctive theoretical contribution came in 2022 with the "Turing trap." He pointed out that AI development has long been shaped by a deep-seated design bias: trying to make machines imitate humans and replace humans, with passing the Turing test treated as the ultimate goal. Yet this design philosophy pushes technology onto a path of labor substitution rather than labor augmentation. When the research target is narrowly focused on making AI "like humans," automation becomes the default route, while augmentation is marginalized. Brynjolfsson argues that we should deliberately shift the research direction away from "making machines do what people do" and toward "making people do what they could not do before."

In 2025, he and his collaborators published "The Canary in the Coal Mine," a data-rich study tracing the early labor-market effects of AI. The findings show that freelancers and platform workers were hit first. These workers, lacking organizational protection, were the first to feel the competitive pressure from generative AI. In fields such as translation, copywriting, and basic programming, freelancers saw observable declines in orders and income.

At the same time, aggregate employment data at the enterprise level did not collapse, suggesting that AI's substitution effect unfolded gradually inside organizations. Brynjolfsson is currently advancing an "AI economic dashboard" project that uses big data to track task shifts across thousands of occupations in the United States in real time. The latest monitoring suggests that in 2026, the pace of organizational change finally began to catch up with the pace of technology, and firms are undergoing a risky transition from "piloting AI" to "reengineering processes."

## 4. Andrew McAfee: Technological Optimism, Institutional Anxiety

Andrew McAfee is Brynjolfsson's long-time academic partner. He serves as principal research scientist at MIT Sloan and is also a co-founder of the MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy. Compared with Brynjolfsson's more academic style, McAfee is better at translating complex economic findings into language that business leaders and policymakers can understand. He is active in *Harvard Business Review*, TED talks, and his personal columns.

His core position can be summarized as "technological optimism with institutional anxiety." On the one hand, he believes technological progress has generally expanded the size of the economy. On the other hand, he is extremely alert to how the gains are distributed. After *The Second Machine Age*, he and Brynjolfsson co-authored *Machine, Platform, Crowd*, further arguing that the digital economy has a striking feature: growth is increasingly concentrated in a small number of platform companies and superstar firms, while ordinary workers receive a shrinking share of that growth. That winner-take-all tendency may accelerate in the AI era.

McAfee therefore does not simply take the position that AI will create more jobs. His concern is different: if institutions and policy do not intervene proactively, the value created by AI will be highly concentrated in the hands of capital owners and a small number of highly skilled workers.

## 5. Philippe Aghion: Creative Destruction and Institutional Absorption

The last of the five is Philippe Aghion. He teaches at the College de France and the London School of Economics (LSE). In 2025, he won the Nobel Prize in Economics for his groundbreaking contributions to innovation and growth theory, especially the Schumpeterian growth model he developed with Peter Howitt. The core concept of that theory is "creative destruction": new technologies and new firms inevitably destroy old industrial structures and job patterns, and that destruction is precisely what drives long-run economic growth. Importantly, Aghion's claim is not "the economy recovers after destruction," but rather "destruction itself is the engine of growth." The distinction matters.

Applied to AI, Aghion's analytical path is clear. AI will certainly destroy many existing jobs, but as long as that destruction is accompanied by enough innovation and sufficient institutional flexibility, it can also give rise to new industries and new employment. The key variable is not how powerful the technology is, but whether the institutional environment can absorb the destruction. Is market competition sufficiently strong? Can the education system adapt in time? Is the social safety net robust enough?

Aghion has also recently been using large language models themselves as tools for economic research, exploring how LLMs can support large-scale social surveys and text analysis. In other words, he is not only studying how AI affects the economy, but also using AI to redefine the methods of economics research itself.

## Conclusion: Not a Single Camp

Placed side by side, the views of these five scholars do not form a single camp. Acemoglu is a calm skeptic who believes the economic impact of AI is being seriously overstated. Autor is a conditional optimist who thinks AI may benefit the middle class, but only if it is developed in the right direction. Brynjolfsson is a structuralist who argues that the real issue is not AI itself, but what humans choose to use it for. McAfee focuses on distributive justice and worries that value will be captured by a small group. Aghion is a historicist who believes creative destruction will ultimately do its work, but only with institutional support.

They agree in some places and diverge in others, but they share a deeper consensus: the impact of technological change on the labor market is not just an engineering problem. It is an economic one. It depends on institutional design, policy choices, market structure, and corporate decisions, not simply on the upper bound of model capability. In today's noisy environment, we clearly need more diverse voices.
